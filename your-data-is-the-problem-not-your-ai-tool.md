## The Wrong Diagnosis

Most SMEs who try AI and get poor results blame the technology. The tool produced nonsense. The output was generic. It made things up. The summary was useless. And so the conclusion is that AI is overhyped, or that it works for big companies with big resources but not for a business like theirs.

Usually, the diagnosis is wrong. The tool is not the problem. The data sitting behind it is.

## What AI Actually Needs to Work

AI tools are powerful, but they are not independent. They work with what you give them. When the information going in is incomplete, inconsistent, or scattered across a dozen different places, the output reflects that. The intelligence is only as good as the inputs.

For most SMEs, the inputs are a mess. Not through any fault of the business owner, but because data management has never needed to be a priority before. The [ONS Business Insights Survey](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/businessservices/bulletins/businessinsightsandimpactontheukeconomy/2october2025) shows that even among UK businesses actively using AI, data quality remains the most commonly cited barrier to getting value from it. Client records are spread across email threads, a spreadsheet someone built three years ago, and a CRM that was never quite configured correctly. Invoices are in one system, correspondence is in another, and the information that would actually be useful to an AI tool has never been consolidated in a form it can read.

This is the data debt that most businesses are carrying, and it is the main reason AI trials fail.

## The Three Most Common Data Problems

### Fragmented records

Information about a single client or project lives in multiple places that have never been connected. A proposal is in Google Drive, the email thread is in Gmail, the invoice is in [Xero](https://www.xero.com/uk/), and the notes from the last three calls are in someone's notebook. An AI tool given any one of these has no context. Given all of them without structure, it cannot do much better.

### Outdated information

Databases that have not been cleaned in years contain contacts who have moved on, addresses that no longer exist, and relationship records that reflect how things were rather than how they are. An AI tool asked to work from this data will produce outputs that feel off, because they are built on information that no longer reflects reality. It is worth noting that [GDPR's accuracy principle](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-protection-principles/accuracy/) also requires personal data to be kept up to date, so a data clean-up serves compliance as well as AI readiness.

### Implicit knowledge

The most valuable information in many small businesses lives in the heads of the people who run it, not in any system. The preferred way to approach a particular client. The reason a certain project was scoped the way it was. The context behind a decision that was made eighteen months ago. None of this is accessible to an AI tool because it was never written down.

## Why This Matters Before You Spend a Penny on Tools

The order in which businesses approach AI adoption makes an enormous difference to the outcome. Businesses that buy tools first and ask questions later almost always encounter the data problem. They spend money, try to use the tool, get poor results, conclude AI is not for them, and stop.

Businesses that start with a clear-eyed assessment of where their data actually is, and what state it is in, can make much more targeted decisions. They know which workflows have the data quality to support AI right now, which ones need a small amount of remediation first, and which ones are genuinely not ready yet. That knowledge means every pound spent on tooling goes to a workflow where it will actually work.

## What Good Data Readiness Looks Like

You do not need perfect data to get value from AI. Very few businesses have perfect data. What you need is data that is good enough for the specific task you are trying to automate, in a format the tool can access, and updated regularly enough to produce reliable outputs.

For some workflows, that bar is already met. For others, a few straightforward changes make the difference. Moving information from email threads into a structured log. Running a contact database through a basic deduplication process. Setting up a simple template so that project notes are captured consistently going forward. None of these are large projects. They are the kind of changes that take hours rather than weeks, and they unlock disproportionate value.
