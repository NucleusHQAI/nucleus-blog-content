You bought the tool. You set it up. You showed the team how to use it and there were nods of enthusiasm around the room. Three weeks later, barely anyone is touching it.

This is one of the most common experiences businesses have with AI implementations. It is not a sign that the tool is bad, or that your team is resistant to change. It is the predictable outcome of a specific problem, and it has a solution.

## The Honeymoon Period Is Real

The first week or two of a new AI tool usually goes well. Curiosity drives usage. People try things out, share examples, and the early results often are impressive. A task that used to take an hour gets done in ten minutes. Someone generates a first draft that would have taken them an afternoon.

This creates a misleading benchmark. The tool is being used for the easy, high-visibility wins. The examples that get shared are the ones that worked. The ones that did not quite land get quietly set aside, and nobody adjusts their expectations accordingly.

## What Happens in Week Three

By the third or fourth week, the novelty has worn off. The initial wins were real, but applying the tool to actual daily work is harder than the demos suggested. Getting consistently good outputs requires learning how to frame requests well. Existing processes have not been restructured to incorporate the tool. The path of least resistance is to go back to doing things the old way.

Usage drops. The subscription continues. At the end of the quarter, the question is whether to renew.

This is not a failure of the tool or the team. It is a structural problem. Without someone actively managing the transition from trial to embedded use, most implementations stall here.

## Why Month Three Is the Real Measure

Businesses that break through this plateau typically do so in the six to twelve weeks after initial deployment. They experience a second curve of genuine productivity gain as:

- Staff build confidence and learn to get better outputs from the tool
- Workflows are restructured around the tool rather than bolted on top of them
- Time savings start compounding because the tool is being used consistently, not occasionally
- The team identifies secondary use cases that were not in the original plan

The businesses that reach this point did not get there by accident. They had a plan for the difficult middle period, not just for the launch.

## The One Thing That Changes the Outcome

The single biggest predictor of whether an AI implementation delivers lasting value is whether someone takes responsibility for the integration, not just the purchase.

This means:

- Identifying which specific tasks the tool will own, not just telling the team to "use AI more"
- Building the tool into documented processes so it becomes a default, not a choice
- Running follow-up sessions with staff after the initial training, not just one launch event
- Having a point of contact who can troubleshoot when things do not work as expected

In larger businesses this might be an internal project manager or a technology lead. In most SMEs, this support needs to come from outside, at least for the initial implementation period.

## The Middle Period Is Where Projects Die

The period between initial deployment and embedded use is where most AI projects quietly collapse. Not dramatically, not with a formal decision to abandon the tool. They just fade. Usage becomes occasional, then rare, then the subscription is cancelled and the conclusion is that AI is "not quite there yet for us."

That conclusion is almost never accurate. It reflects what happens when a capable tool meets a gap in implementation support.

The businesses that invested in proper implementation and came back six months later with strong results are not unusual. They are the norm among businesses that approached it correctly.
