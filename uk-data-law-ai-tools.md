The UK's Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 received Royal Assent earlier this year. Most small business owners have not heard of it. Many of those who have heard of it assume it does not apply to them. Both assumptions are worth revisiting.

If your business uses AI tools that process customer data, staff data, or any personal information, this legislation changes what is expected of you. The rules are not theoretical. They come with accountability requirements, and the ICO has been explicit that AI governance is a priority enforcement area.

## What the Act Actually Does

The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 builds on the foundations of UK GDPR but extends the rules around how automated decision-making and AI processing can be used with personal data. The key changes that matter for most small businesses:

**Automated decisions carry stricter accountability requirements.** If an AI tool is making or significantly influencing decisions that affect individuals, such as flagging customer risk, filtering job applications, or personalising communications, you now need to be able to explain and document how those decisions are being made.

**Data residency has increased significance.** The Act reinforces expectations around where personal data is processed and stored. Using a US-based AI tool that handles customer data on American servers is no longer a grey area. It carries explicit compliance implications.

**Consent and transparency obligations are tighter.** Using AI to process customer data for purposes beyond what was originally stated when the data was collected requires clear justification, and in many cases renewed consent.

## The Problem with "Just Using ChatGPT"

The most common data risk in small businesses right now is not a malicious breach. It is an employee pasting customer data or personal information into a public-facing AI tool without thinking about where that data goes.

ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and similar tools, when used with default settings and without a business data agreement in place, process the content you give them on their own infrastructure. That infrastructure is typically in the United States. When that content includes names, email addresses, financial details, or any other personal data, you have likely created a compliance issue before anyone has even thought to ask the question.

The Act makes the consequences of this clearer and the accountability more traceable.

## What "Safe Pair of Hands" Actually Means

When Nucleus AI recommends AI tools for a client, UK data residency is a core part of the evaluation criteria. This is not a box-ticking exercise. It is a recognition that small business owners have enough to manage without building a compliance liability into their operations.

Practically, this means:

- Prioritising tools with UK or EU data processing options
- Checking whether enterprise data agreements are in place and what they cover
- Avoiding the default assumption that a well-known brand means a compliant tool
- Documenting what data is being processed and why, in plain language

The AI Readiness Assessment includes a review of the tools your business currently uses and flags any that carry meaningful data risk. The goal is not to remove useful tools but to make sure you are using them in a way that is defensible.

## Why This Matters More Than It Did Twelve Months Ago

The ICO has stated publicly that AI governance is a priority enforcement area for the next two years. Small businesses are not exempt from this scrutiny. The Act provides a clearer legal framework, which means complaints and investigations now have sharper teeth.

The businesses that will navigate this well are not necessarily the ones with the largest legal teams. They are the ones that took the time to understand what tools they are using, what data those tools handle, and what agreements are in place. That is genuinely achievable for most SMEs without significant cost or overhead.

## Where to Start

The first step is not a legal audit or a complete overhaul of your toolstack. It is getting a clear picture of what you are currently using and what data is involved.

Most businesses are surprised by what that review surfaces. Tools they assumed were compliant often are not, at least not in their default configuration. Tools they were nervous about are often fine with minor adjustments. The landscape is more navigable than the headlines suggest, but you do need to look at it.

Starting with a clear picture of your current exposure is the most practical first move, and the one most likely to keep you ahead of a problem rather than scrambling to respond to one.
